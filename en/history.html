Schumann resonance history

The peak frequency over the past days, reading by reading

This page shows our own series of Schumann resonance measurements. A program reads the public spectrogram from the Tomsk station, finds the peak of the fundamental, and records the frequency and the intensity. The result is kept, and that is what appears here.

The program is scheduled every 30 minutes, but it runs on GitHub Actions, which executes scheduled jobs when capacity allows. In practice that works out at roughly one reading every hour and a half. We would rather say so than advertise a cadence that is not met.

No public source publishes these values as numbers. We checked file by file: the station publishes an image and nothing else. Anyone showing a live "F1 = 7.70 Hz" is reading it from the image, as we do, or inventing it. The difference is in saying so, and in showing the method.

The full series

Each point is one reading of the spectrogram. The line breaks wherever there was no trustworthy measurement.

The series is still filling up. Measurements began being stored on 16 August 2026, and the chart appears once there are eight readings. If you arrived before that, come back later.

Is the frequency rising?

It is the question that comes up most often on this subject, and the series answers it better than any explanation. The fundamental oscillates, every day, typically between about 7.6 and 8.1 Hz. It rises at night and falls during the day, because the effective height of the ionosphere changes with sunlight and the cavity between the Earth and the ionosphere changes size with it.

That daily oscillation is the normal behaviour of the system. It is not a signal of anything, and above all it is not a trend: look at the 7.83 Hz line on the chart and notice that the series crosses it up and down continuously, without drifting away from it.

The idea that the frequency is climbing towards 40 Hz comes from misreading the spectrogram itself. The vertical axis of the image runs to nearly 40 Hz because it also shows the higher modes, around 14.3, 20.8, 27.3 and 33.8 Hz. Seeing activity at the top of the image means seeing another band of the same chart, not the fundamental moving.

What a refused reading means

Not every half hour produces a measurement. The chart shows gaps, and those gaps are deliberate.

A reading is refused when the Tomsk receiver is saturated, which happens roughly one hour in four and fills the whole band with white; when the peak sits against the edge of the search band, a sign that what was found is probably not the fundamental; when the colours in the image do not match the published scale; and when the image changes dimensions, which is how a change of format at the station is detected.

In those cases a row is stored saying there was no reading, with the reason. Nothing is interpolated and nothing is filled in. Joining the two sides of a gap would draw a measurement that never existed, and that is precisely what separates a data series from a pretty chart.

The raw data

The series is available as a JSON file, with no registration and no key, for anyone who wants to verify it, analyse it, or use it in other work:

Download the series as JSON →

The format is a list of rows with four fields: the timestamp in UTC, the peak frequency in hertz, the intensity on the 0 to 100 colour scale, and the state of the reading. A null frequency means the reading was refused, and the state says why. The last thirty days are kept.

If you use this data, we would appreciate attribution to this site and to the original source, the Space Observing System at Tomsk State University. The intensity comes from the spectrogram colour scale and is not calibrated in picotesla, because the station does not publish the calibration: comparing values between days is legitimate, converting them into physical units is not.
